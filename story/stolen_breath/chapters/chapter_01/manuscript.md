# Chapter 1 // Ren

> **The long pass.** The guard's slow walk from the corner back to the gate. They timed it at seven minutes and most of another.

***

I am late, so the clinic-lane market gets me at a quick pace. Silt ticks on the zinc and sticks in the cloth I pulled up for the walk. Faces under the cloth are bare. The grit rides the cloth until the hour passes and people take it off. They are out anyway, because the night will freeze hard enough to push them indoors, and a post that waits will be selling to nobody.

The mash has the wide part of the lane. Dense green cakes sit on a hot sheet, and the steam puts salt and copper on my tongue as I pass. A man takes two without slowing. My hands stay empty. Drew enjoys late more than she enjoys mash, and I still have to reach her.

Ash-bread is the next post, grub flour gone dark in the pan. The woman there already paid me, a loaf and a corner of roof, for the hour a sweep comes back.

"Dusk," she says.

"Today," I say, and I am past the loaves. "Tomorrow costs you another one."

That loaf is supper, and supper is as much pleasure as this lane gets to hold. What buys the next one is a leak the dome did not mean to give us. T.J. sits under the street and takes the traffic as it passes. A post change, the destination on a package, a service some office requested: those notes were supposed to reach a Directorate subscriber and stop. One of them, now and then, is tomorrow's bread. The rest of the traffic we let go.

He is the eyes and the ears. He hears a patrol before the lane does, and he does not come up. Drew keeps the sets, the tangs, and the wire on her bench, so his ears have something to hear through. Tsune says which note is worth selling and which hour I walk it. I am the quick fingers, and the shadow on the roofs and in the alleys, there with the word and gone with the loaf.

Tonight's job is a service door on the wall, still live. Drew has the tang for it on the bench, and I am not there yet.

Sulfur-fowl turns on a wire over the next coals. Fat snaps. I keep the pace. The link clicks against my jaw, T.J. close to the mic.

"Foot patrol. Four. North mouth. They are in the lane. Exit."

Boots and a gray sleeve fill the north mouth. I take the gap beside the candy post. The tin sits on the edge of the board, dark pieces, and the woman who keeps it is watching those boots. My hand takes one. The candy is hard, molasses and vanilla and cinnamon, and it sticks to a molar while the lane goes behind me.

A couple hundred feet of zinc and I have Mercer's stair. The climb leaves a dry catch behind my ribs. I pretend it is the stair.

Drew is at the bench in the back, dark hair cut at the jaw, grease worked into the burn on her left hand. A dead keypad sits in the vise. Tsune's bodkin, the short knife she wears through her braid, is in two pieces on the bench because the small metal end of it has to fit a keypad and Drew has been matching that end to the dead one. The tang is in her fingers. The blade she twisted off to get at it lies on the rag, longer than the tang and duller than it looks in Tsune's hair.

"You're late," Drew says.

The candy clicks against the tooth. "Market. T.J. pulled me. Four on the north mouth."

She looks at my jaw. "Swallow it before Tsune decides you robbed her lane."

"The bread woman has her dusk. I told her tomorrow costs."

"You sell today." She tips her chin at the stool. "Sit, before you fall over and make me a nurse."

I sit. The catch comes again on the way down, and Drew hears it the way she hears a bad bearing. She does not make it a conversation. The cup she puts by my hand is hot. I drink, and the heat sits in my chest where the catch was, which is as close as either of us gets to talking about it. The candy goes down with it, sweet enough that I know I did not pay.

Tsune comes in from the lane without a sound I can use. She looks at the piece in Drew's fingers before she looks at me. The bodkin is through her braid and short the blade that is lying on the rag, the grip waiting for Drew to be finished with it.

"Roof was clean," she says. "Ren."

"Here."

"You ran the market."

"T.J. called it dirty. I listened."

She nods once, which is the whole of the praise I ever get from her, and it still lands warm under the ribs. She pulls the goggles down onto the collar and looks at the keypad in the vise.

"Talk," she says.

T.J. is already in the room, which means the set on the bench is live and he is under a street he will not come up from. Young voice. Close to the mic, the same closeness he used to pull me out of the crowd. "Twelve long passes. Same boots, same corner, same lazy turn back to the gate. Seven point eight from the corner to the mouth. The camera on that door looks at the bridge joint. It does not look at the keypad."

"And the wire," Tsune says.

"Already mine. I use it for the gate paint. You seat a jack in that keypad and the picture comes down the door's own wire. I read it here. You hold still and the copy sits in your slate. I don't need you to look at a screen. I need the jack to stay seated."

Drew turns the tang so the contacts catch the lamp. "It matches the scrap. A live door will take it or it will scream. I can't promise which until you ask it."

"We have asked prowlers," Tsune says. "We have caught a broadcast when somebody got lazy on an open set. We have not put a jack in a door that is still on the wall."

"That's the point," Drew says. "A prowler tells us where a truck is, and a broadcast tells us what a fool said out loud. The security net tells us which doors they walk and which cameras are dead. I can shut this clinic for the hour that matters, instead of for the hour I guess."

I know that hour. Last month the guess was wrong, and Mercer spent it in the front with the lamp off and a hand over a boy's mouth so the boy would not cough while boots went past the door. Nobody has told him about tonight. He is in there now, boiling water, trusting us to be the kind of noise that does not bring a patrol into his front room. If the jack works, he gets a quieter night and a lie about how we knew.

"I can stand at the keypad," I say.

Tsune looks at me then, finished, the way she looks when she has already had the argument with herself and I was not invited.

"You can stand on the roof that sees the corner. You count the boots. If the pass breaks the habit, you say it in the same breath. You do not come to the door. A second body on that bridge is a picture, and a picture paints this room."

*Let me be close enough to pull you off if it screams early.* I am surprised the set does not pick it up. She has already heard it, from the shape of my mouth or from knowing me, and she has thrown it out.

"Drew could..."

"No," Drew says. "I'm not your swap."

I toe the silt I tracked in. "Seven point eight. If I lose the count, you pull."

"If you lose the count, I already pulled." She takes the tang from Drew, draws the short grip out of her braid, and seats the blade back on with a twist I have watched a hundred times. The bodkin goes through the hair again until the steel sits against her skull. "We go at the silt. The pass loves its habit, and the patrol can have the market. Ren, roof. You call the corner boot and then you call nothing else unless the number changes."

"Copy."

Mercer coughs in the front room, an old sound. Tsune kills the bench lamp. Drew wraps the dead keypad in a rag and puts it under the bench, where a man boiling water will not ask what it is. I pull the cloth back up. The lane I came through is still buying the last of the day before the freeze.

She goes out first, collar up, and takes the lanes toward the sand. I take the roofs. Zinc, a gap, zinc again, and the market noise is gone by the time the belt opens in front of me. From the third roof I can see the bare sand, the red cluster at the gate, a short bridge well off the mouth, and the service door, a plain slab with nobody on it. The bridge is empty. I set the link against my mouth and keep the number in my teeth.

Silt shifts under the bridge. She is low in it, and then I lose her. The hooks take the lip and she is on the landing, one hand on the hook. She does not look at my roof. She doesn't have to. The boots have not hit the corner yet.

When they do, I tell her. Until then I keep still. *Count.*

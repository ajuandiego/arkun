# The Quota Calculation // Interlude

The beef is real. Julian Ward cuts it on the bias, the way the cook asked him to, and the juice stays on the plate instead of running. Strawberries sit in a bowl that has never been in a silt wind. His daughter, seven, has one on her thumb and is explaining a horse.

"She is white," the girl says. "Except the ears. The ears are gray because the program said a horse needs a serious part."

"A serious horse," he says.

"She lives by the fountain. I can see her from the school glass if I stand on the bench, and they tell me to get down, and then I stand on the bench."

His wife laughs. She is pregnant, and the shield-light makes a line along her collar, and she has been beautiful in that light for the whole season he has been signing papers at this table. The penthouse faces the inner courts. She wants the athletic field wider. The gardeners want a margin for the roses. Both of those wants are on the quarterly sheet in his lap, under the linen, where a child's strawberry will not mark them.

"The balance holds if the outer band gives," he says, to the sheet.

She looks at the sheet the way she looks at weather. "Will anyone be angry?"

The question means the rooms that can reach this one. He knows the other answer and he does not set it on the table. Sector 09, the downwind wards. A street and a half of spill, pulled back. The inner field gets its width. Four thousand people lose the air that was leaning over their roofs. Afternoons that were only copper at the edges become silt. Cellars that were damp become pools after dark. He has read the note under the number. He does not say the note. The strawberries are cold and sweet and his daughter is waiting to hear about the ears.

"No one who is invited to dinner."

He signs. The pen is heavy. The signature is small, the same size it was when the number was a garden. He wipes his mouth with the linen. He leans over and kisses his daughter's forehead. She smells like the fruit. She takes it as affection, because it has the shape of affection, and because he is her father in a room where the lamps are kind.

"My blood survives," he says, soft. "The rest is arithmetic."

"Is the horse still white if I go tomorrow?" she asks.

"The ears stay gray." He pours his wife another glass. The wine tastes like the year on the bottle. The sheet goes into the case. The case shuts. He cuts his daughter another strawberry and repeats the horse's name.

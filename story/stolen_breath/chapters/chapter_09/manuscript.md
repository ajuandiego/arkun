# Chapter 9 // Jeffrey

Nine years before the present.

> *Write down the cough. Write down nothing else.*

***

The grate over us is the workshop floor. Boots cross it one at a time. Silt ticks the zinc and drops through, and then the boots stop long enough for a man to look at the same corner twice. They are counting the room. Men who count a room find the way down.

She hears it. Her eyes come up over the sleeve.

"How many?"

"Enough of them, and they're taking their time." I keep my face on the bench. If one of them is already on the ladder, a look up tells him someone is awake under the grate. "Keep that cough in your sleeve. They'll hear it before they hear their own boots."

*Don't write this down.*

The lamp is the only warm thing in the room, copper light off the street and the smell of solvent off the bench. Tsunari sits on the stool with her sleeve over her mouth. She is seventeen, and the cough has been in that breath for a month. The city is still calling it dust. I wrote the cough down and nothing else. A page on this bench can be carried out, and a page will not stand between her and those boots.

Five glass dishes sit in the lamp. Each one has a film growing across the glass, and the light sits wrong on it. I count them the way I count a stair I may have to run. The one nearest my hand is the only one I can afford to say out loud.

"This one stops your cough," I tell her. "I call it the culture. Say it once, so I know you heard me."

"The culture stops the cough." Her voice is thin, and furious, which is how I know the month has not finished her. "What are the other four?"

"It's better if you don't know."

"You made me count them."

"I know. You can count them. That's all I wanted." I cap the one I named. A dish can leave this room in a coat, and a note gets read by a man who was never in the room. My hands are steady on the glass. Four names said to my daughter are four names a stranger on the grate can take out of her.

She coughs into the sleeve. The sound is small and ugly in a room this quiet. When she drops the cloth, her eyes are on the dishes, not on me. She reaches for the far one. I catch her wrist. The glass ticks the bench and settles. Her pulse is fast under my fingers, hot with the cough. I let go before the hold can turn into a comfort she will hate me for later.

"I can read a film," she says.

"Don't read it. Leave the glass alone." If she reads what is growing on the glass, she has the name, and a name is a thing they can take out of her.

"You're using me," she says.

"Yes. I am. I'd rather you hate me for it now than thank me later."

"I wasn't going to thank you."

She looks at the inner door. Sora is behind it, ten, asleep, a lump under a blanket I checked an hour ago and will not check again. She is well. If I open that door I put her on a page those boots can take. The cough will not wait for some other body, and it will not be the girl under that blanket. *Don't open it.*

"Sora isn't part of this. She stays asleep."

Something small turns over behind the inner door, a blanket and a child's breath. My hand is on the latch, and I do not remember putting it there. I take it off. *Leave her the night.*

"I know." Tsunari pulls the sleeve down. The cough stays in the room with us. "Do it before I decide to be brave and stupid."

I do it. She puts her free hand on the stool leg and holds until the wood complains. Her arm is hot from the cough and too thin for the month. The culture goes in first, because that is the sentence I can live with, and I tell her it is the culture while the plunger moves. She watches my hand, not the vein. The second draw has no mark on the glass. She watches that one harder. It goes in after, into the daughter who is already failing, because a body that cannot name what is in it is harder to steal than a slide. *No name on the glass, and none in her mouth.* If I stop, the dishes leave with the boots, she keeps the cough, and Sora wakes to neither of us. My hands keep the work. The part of me that wants to put the syringe down does not get a hand.

"That one," she says, when the second is empty. "Say it."

"It's better if you don't know."

I make her stand. She sways and catches the bench. The legs are already taking more of her weight than they did when she sat down.

"They feel wrong," she says.

"I know." I keep my hand on the bench, not on her. "Your skin, your eyes, your legs. They'll change. You'll run colder than you do now. You'll find out what that means as you grow. Don't let anyone learn these things about you."

She looks at her legs. "You could have written that down and let me read it when you were gone."

"It's better if it isn't written down." A page lasts longer than I do, and she would be the one they asked to explain it.

"One more, and I need it exact. If it cannot go home, it cooks."

She frowns. The frown is the same one she uses on a sum that will not balance. "If it cannot go home, it cooks. That's a bad culture."

"Again. Keep going until you can say it without stopping to think. You don't have to understand it."

She says it again, angry and exact, the way she says a thing she intends to keep and not forgive. She can repeat it, and she does not know what it is for. *Good.* An understanding can be pulled out of a girl on a stair. A sentence she can repeat without knowing why is only a habit, and a habit is a poor prize.

"Mercer will teach you the rest," I say. "The clinic, the knife, the trade."

"There are notes. I watched you keep a bench."

"I got rid of them. If he asks what the other dishes were, you tell him the truth. You don't know."

"If I guess, and I'm right, will you nod?"

"No." A nod would be the name. I would rather have burned this bench yesterday.

"Where are you going?"

"I'm taking the crawler. If I make the engine loud, they'll follow me instead of staying here."

Boots leave the grate and hit the wooden stair. They are closer. I take her face in my hands and let go in the same breath, because a long touch becomes a goodbye and a goodbye becomes a speech. Her skin is already cooler than the fever I just had under my fingers. *Already.* I do not say it.

"If this doesn't work, I put a needle in you for nothing. That scares me." The stair creaks under a careful weight. "If it works, the men on that stair won't kill you. They'll keep you. That scares me more. Don't show off. Don't be brave where they can put a collar on you."

"You already did."

"Then don't make it easy for them. And get out of this room." I put her on the alley stair. Their boots are on the wooden stair inside. This stair comes out in the lane. "Left in the lane. Mercer's door. Don't stop."

She looks at the inner door. "She's ten."

"She's asleep. If you open that door, the two of you are still here when they come down."

"I'm not leaving her with them on the stair."

"Yes, you are." I stand between her and the latch. My hand stays off it. *Leave her the night.* "Go."

She goes. I hear the alley door, and then I wait until I cannot hear her.

I tip all five dishes into the solvent. The films cloud and die. The inner door stays shut. The culture is already in her, and so is the answer I alone know.

The alley is empty of her when I come up. Night in the lane is the hard cold of the lee, copper still in the air, silt gone to frost on the zinc. I walk. If I run, they look for a second runner, and the second runner is her. The crawler's lamps are on. I make the engine a rude, trackable noise, and I leave it that way. *Louder.*

Shouts behind me. A man calls a direction and gets no answer he likes. A light swings off the workshop door and onto the lane, and for one second it could still choose the corner she took. I hold the engine open until the light gives up the door and takes the noise instead. They follow. *Stay the louder fact.*

I do not look back at the alley mouth. A look is a turn, and a turn would put their light on the corner I sent her around. The crawler takes the south road loud on purpose, and the strike team comes with it, away from the inner door, away from that corner. I do not give the road a name. A name is a direction, and she is still close enough to be the one they take. Tonight the job is the noise and it's working.

# Chapter 18 // Sora

> *The new mist will help the people outside breathe clean again.*

***

The window in my room looks down on the court and out across the bowl, and I stand at it with wet hair until the glass stops fogging. Jasmine grows in the bed under the sill, the real plant, and the building pays to put the same smell in the halls so the stair never goes blank. Nobody picks the court bed down to the stick. The boy from the next stair is already on the painted lines, shouting a score he invented, and the ball stays where he puts it. The air in here does not move unless a person walks through it.

Above the court the shield is a vault of hexagons, even, one rib the same as the next, and the day inside them is green. The canal takes that green and lays gold on it where the towers stand in the water. Faces on the walks are bare. A tram slides the near bank without a wheel-sound I can hear from up here, and the mast in Sector 1 is a slim line the garden towers do not quite hide. It comes up out of the park and stops at the crown. I have the inside, and the inside is enough to want again.

At the far curve the color changes. The hexes there are still ours. Past them the air is copper, and the roofs under it are a texture, not a street I could name. They go on until the haze takes the end of them. I put a hand on the glass. It is cool, and it is the last cool thing the shift will give me.

I badge through with the coat actually white. His mother is on the stair with the baby on her hip, and the baby gets a fist into my cuff and will not give it back.

"Lab day," she says.

"Twelve hours. Keene counts the minutes, and then she counts them again."

"Eat." She breaks a pastry and puts the warm half in my hand. The butter is the real thing, or close enough that I don't ask the kitchen. "They posted us. The permit. He can have the name on the door."

The baby has jam on his mouth and no interest in names. I take the pastry. A posted permit is a luck you do not shrug off. "Tell him the score still has to add."

"He knows." She lifts him until the fist loses my cuff. The boy yells a worse number, delighted with himself. "Go be useful. Some of us are spending the morning you keep filing."

"I'll look at the permit when it's on the glass," I say. "High. Where the stair can see it."

"If I come home mean, I want it where I don't have to hunt." She is already smiling, which is how I know the mean is a story she tells herself so the day has a shape. The baby tries for the cuff again and gets a kiss on the fist instead.

The tram is bright, and the rail is clean enough that I can see the canal under it, gold going to green when the towers let the shield through. Sector 2 passes in garden walls, white coats, a clinic stair with the door already open and a nurse arguing with a boy about shoes. Faces stay bare. I drink from the canteen because the water is real and the shift will not let me later. The woman in the next seat is peeling a pear with a thumbnail and talking to nobody I can see.

"They moved the strings," she says, to the pear, or to me. "Plaza used to start on the downbeat. Now they start wherever the couple feels like stepping out. I missed my stop twice last month watching them."

"Did you mind?"

"I minded the walk." She offers me the next slice. I take it. It tastes like the tree and like the cold room they keep the tree's fruit in. "You lab?"

"Culture. Twelve hours if Keene is in a kind mood, which she counts as the same thing."

She stands at the library stop. "Tell your Keene the downbeat was better."

On the library stair an old man has a girl by the sleeve, or she has him. He is telling her that rain used to make the stone shine, that it came out of a sky with no ribs in it and left the walk dark and then bright. She says say it again. He does, glad to be asked, and he looks up at our ribs as if they might agree to try. Children are already in the canal with an instructor who is pretending not to smile. One of them swallows water and comes up laughing, and the instructor's hand is on the rail, ready, and does not have to grab.

The plaza is playing the same four strings it plays every morning. A couple takes two steps out of the walk when the song gets to the part they like, and the crowd goes around them without making them stop. A stall has the tart fruit that comes through the gate and costs more than it should. A man buys two and gives one to a girl in an academy sash. She eats it on the stair like a prize, juice on her thumb, and she does not wipe it until she has finished. I want the outside to have this. That is the mist, when I say it to myself plain. *Let them have the morning.*

I was ten when they brought me in. They washed my hair until it was only hair. They said my father killed himself after the raid. They said my sister died of the Choke in a gutter, and that I was the one who could be taught. The sister stays in the story they gave me. I have a shift, and a baby with jam on his mouth, and a city that posted a permit before breakfast.

Culture Lab 4 holds the same light as the canal. Warm on the benches. The color the holos mean when they talk about a day worth keeping. The long window on the south wall is hexes again, closer, and if I stand on my toes at the glove rack I can see the canal's last bright reach and then the copper, the same stain I had from my room, only wider. I hang the coat. I glove. The crack in my knuckle is from yesterday's wash. The queue is already moving.

"Thorne. Packet twelve. Don't admire it."

"Yes, ma'am."

Keene does not stay for the thank-you. She is already at the next pair of hands, counting the minute they took to badge. The woman at the bench beside mine does not look up. Her gloves are wet. "If the tray clears, my brother puts in for the second. He says a city that can post a permit can stand one more mouth."

"The tray is a trial."

"It's the closest thing he has." She bumps my elbow with hers, careful of the queue. "File yours clean. Some of us are spending it."

Packet twelve is a telemetry strip from a south post. Commander. Avian. A fever trace, then a flat place in the noise, then the fever again. I do not know the man. I know the form. A spike left sitting is how a number becomes someone in a bed, and the bed is somebody's brother whether the post is mine or not. I flag it. The note is three words. Anomaly. Repeat. Review.

On the bench beside the queue, the mist trial sits in a sealed tray. Supervisor Keene tapped it yesterday and said the line we are allowed to say. The new mist will help the people outside breathe clean again. I believe her. The work is ugly in the hands and kind in the purpose. They told me that when they gave me the coat. The canal this morning did not argue.

The middle bell is not the end. Keene calls it the long half and means we eat where she can see that we come back. Pell from the glove line has the window bench and a pear he should not have brought past the rack. He moves his cup so I can sit.

"If Keene sees the pear she will count it as a spill and then as a moral," he says. "Eat fast."

"A woman on the tram told me the same thing. She also told me to complain about the downbeat."

"The couple." He is pleased to be asked, the way the old man was. "They stepped out early again. I missed nothing, because I was already late, and being late to Keene is a kind of music." He nods at the glass. "You keep looking at it like it might answer."

Up close the hexes have a thickness, and I can read them as lines, because I wash my hands on the hour and I know a line when it repeats. Inside, the light stays green and the canal stays gold. Past the far curve the copper sits on the roofs, and the roofs do not stop. A violet thread shows a long way south, thin, and I don't give it a name. Pell does the looking for both of us and then gives it back.

"Thicker than last month," he says. "My cousin on a gate pass came home with it in his collar. He laughed and shook it out on the mat. My mother did not laugh."

"That's what the tray is for." I say it the way Keene says the line, and then I say it as myself, because the pear is sweet and the court this morning was a real place. "They'll get a morning. Children in the water. A song they can step out of the walk for. The copper is the job, not the city."

"Keene has that speech and she has never finished a pear at this glass." He eats the core, seeds and all, so the bench has nothing left to show her. "You believe her."

"I believe the tray." The crack in my knuckle pulls when I close the hand. "And I believe the cousin shook it out. Both of those can be true while the work gets done."

"File yours clean, then." He stands, cup in the pocket, no pear left to confess. "Some of us are spending the half you use for looking."

The afternoon queue is the dull packets. A greenhouse count. A permit review that is not mine to approve. A fever that is only a fever, flat nowhere, and I let it pass because a flat place is the thing I was taught to stop for. The woman with the brother asks, once, whether the tray moved. It has not. Keene tapped it and said the line, and the seal is the same seal. My gloves wet through at the third wash. The crack stings and I leave it.

"Thorne." Keene, from the door, not unkind. "The long half is over when I say it is over. Packet twelve went up. Don't stand at the glass and redo a flag you already spent."

"I was looking at the canal."

"Look at it from the tram. The canal does not require a glove." She almost smiles. It would cost her a minute, so she doesn't. "Go home before the court lights and tell me tomorrow that you slept."

When the shift bell goes, the tram is full of people who have been on their feet and are glad of the seat anyway. A nurse with a match ticket. Two students splitting the last pastry. The old man from the library stair, asleep on his own shoulder, the girl still holding the sleeve. The copper at the far curve is still there when the tram turns, and then the towers take it, and Sector 2 is green again, gold on the water, jasmine when the doors open at my stop.

The court is lit when I get back. The boy puts a ball in my hands before I can badge the stair.

"You count fair," he says.

"I count like Keene."

He hates that. That is the game. We play until the baby fusses and the woman calls it for the night. She sticks the permit to the glass, high, where the stair can see it, and the hexes over the court hold the last green while the canal goes dark gold underneath.

"So I can see it when I come home mean," she says.

"You won't come home mean."

"I might." She is smiling. The baby is already heavy on her shoulder, jam wiped off. "Then I look at that, and I feed him, and I do the day again."

I eat the meal they issue. The white coat goes on the chair. From the window the mast is a darker line, the copper is still at the far curve, and the roofs out there still have no end I can find. The spike is a number that wanted a second look. The permit is on the glass. I want the morning, and I mean to have it.
